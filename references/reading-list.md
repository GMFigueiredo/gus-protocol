# GUS Protocol — reading list and review status

Developed by Brazilian data and business analyst Gustavo "Gus" Figueiredo.

These readings examine particular design choices, including reasons to question the protocol's initial intuitions. Four supplied PDFs now inform the first revision; [source notes](source-notes.md) document the reviewed passages and instruction changes. Other entries remain suggestions based on bibliographic metadata and available abstracts. Do not present the drafts as a research-validated tutoring system.

The supplied set is sufficient for the present revision. Further readings below are optional. For a later close reading, use the article or chapter text, including relevant figures and methods, rather than its publisher page or abstract alone.

## First batch: six readings

### 1. Making expert reasoning visible

**Allan Collins, John Seely Brown, and Ann Holum (1991), “Cognitive Apprenticeship: Making Thinking Visible.”** [Public article](https://www.aft.org/ae/winter1991/collins_brown_holum).

**Status:** PDF supplied and reviewed for this revision.

**Why:** A foundation for modeling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, and reducing assistance.

**Protocol decision to examine:** How to expose an expert's useful decisions without doing all the thinking, and when to move from demonstration to supported and independent work. This is the best first anchor for the overall teaching approach.

### 2. The learner explains, rather than merely agrees

**Michelene T. H. Chi, Nicholas De Leeuw, Mei-Hung Chiu, and Christian LaVancher (1994), “Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding.”** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3).

**Status:** PDF supplied; study methods, explanation coding, relevant results, and discussion reviewed.

**Why:** Directly relevant to the difference between recognizing a fluent explanation and constructing one.

**Protocol decision to examine:** Which self-explanation prompts are useful, what they reveal, and how to avoid turning every interaction into a performative teach-back. Read the actual task and learner population before generalizing.

### 3. A challenge to questioning and discovery as defaults

**Paul A. Kirschner, John Sweller, and Richard E. Clark (2006), “Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work: An Analysis of the Failure of Constructivist, Discovery, Problem-Based, Experiential, and Inquiry-Based Teaching.”** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1).

**Status:** Not supplied; optional further reading. Sweller's supplied chapter already provides a basis for the present guidance changes.

**Why:** A counterweight to an unqualified Socratic approach, especially when novices lack prerequisites.

**Protocol decision to examine:** When the agent should stop asking and explain or demonstrate. Treat this as an argument to evaluate in context, not a universal prohibition on inquiry.

### 4. Conversation is not automatically cognitive engagement

**Michelene T. H. Chi and Ruth Wylie (2014), “The ICAP Framework: Linking Cognitive Engagement to Active Learning Outcomes.”** [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/00461520.2014.965823).

**Status:** Not supplied; optional further reading. Do not cite it as a full-text source reviewed in this revision.

**Why:** Offers distinctions between passive, active, constructive, and interactive engagement that can help interrogate apparent participation.

**Protocol decision to examine:** Whether the buddy is generating an inference or integrating ideas, rather than merely taking turns. Do not turn these categories into automatic scores based on message length or speaking time.

### 5. Voice can also make learning harder

**John Sweller (2011), “Cognitive Load Theory,” chapter 2 in _Psychology of Learning and Motivation_, volume 55, pp. 37–76.**

**Status:** Supplied as `Cognitive-Load-Sweller-2011.pdf`; instructional-effects sections reviewed. This chapter substitutes for the previously suggested Leahy and Sweller journal article and directly discusses the relevant modality and transient-information findings. The original article is not treated as separately read.

**Why:** Spoken information disappears unless retained or replayed; extended verbal instructions can overload memory. Voice's conversational convenience does not establish an educational advantage.

**Protocol decisions informed:** The number of unfamiliar relationships in an explanation, worked examples and partial completion, expertise reversal and fading, integrated representations, redundancy, and persistent support for complex spoken instructions. Check the conditions studied before translating findings to an AI conversation.

### 6. Assisted performance versus later independent learning

**Hamsa Bastani et al. (2025), “Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics.”** [Article DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122).

**Status:** Supplied article's main text reviewed. Per Gus's direction, the correction is outside this conceptual revision and is not a required additional reading. The supplementary appendix was not supplied or reviewed.

**Why:** Studies the distinction central to GUS: performance with AI support versus performance after that support is removed, and the effect of tutor design.

**Protocol decisions informed:** Distinguish supported performance from subsequent use with less assistance; verify reference solutions and hints; do not infer learning from confidence or conversational activity. The tutor intervention combined several changes and did not establish a positive unassisted-exam advantage over the control, so do not attribute a general learning benefit to a single prompting rule.

## How to use the readings together

Compare the guidance critique with productive-failure research rather than choosing a slogan. Examine who the learners were, their prior knowledge, what support was supplied, and what outcomes were measured. A productive initial attempt followed by well-designed instruction is different from leaving someone to guess indefinitely.

For each source, retain: the specific claim it supports, the relevant passage, the setting and method, its limits, and the instruction it would change. Keep these notes in working references, not as caveats appended to every learner response.

The LFI inspiration from Molly Graham's “Lazy F***ing Interns” and Jobs to Be Done remains part of the project's development history. Those sources help frame ownership and purpose; they do not by themselves establish an instructional method for school or university learners.
